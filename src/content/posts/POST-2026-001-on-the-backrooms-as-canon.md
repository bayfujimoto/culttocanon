The Backrooms emerged in 2019 as a single image and a single caption on an imageboard. By 2022 it had accreted a transmedia mythology — analog horror video series, fan wikis, found-footage shorts on YouTube, and tabletop derivatives — without any single canonical text to anchor it. This is, on the older terms of canon-formation, a structural impossibility: there is no original. And yet there is, increasingly, a canon.

What does it mean for a tradition to canonize *the absence of an origin*? This essay takes the question seriously rather than dismissing it as a category mistake.

## The image as nucleus

The image is famously simple: yellowed walls, fluorescent overhead lighting, damp carpet, no people. The caption claims one has "noclipped out of reality." Everything that follows — Kane Pixels' video series, the SCP-style additions, the proliferation of "levels" — is a reading of the image. The mythology is hermeneutic in the same way that biblical exegesis is hermeneutic: there is no canon without commentary, and the commentary itself becomes canon.

## What follows

The argument the rest of the piece would make: that the Backrooms reverses the order of canonization that Frank Kermode describes in *The Classic* (1975). For Kermode, the canonical text is the *origin* whose authority radiates outward through commentary; for the Backrooms, the origin is *empty*, and authority accrues at the periphery first.
