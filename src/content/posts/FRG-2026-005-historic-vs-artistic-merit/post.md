A work must have sufficient artistic merit to be canonized; historical significance is not enough. The claim is intuitive, and it is not wrong so much as under-described. It assumes we already know three things we do not: what artistic merit is, independent of the judgments that attribute it; what a canon is, such that a single gate stands at its entrance; and, finally, who is the gatekeeper. Each assumption dissolves on contact. What survives the dissolution is a better question: not whether a work _deserves_ the canon, but which canon, consecrated by which institution, measuring which kind of value, and what the act of consecration does to the work once it is inside.

## The two merits are not rivals

The intuitive framing sets historic merit against artistic merit as though they were two properties of a single object competing for one throne. They are not. They are the objects of two distinct regimes of value, adjudicated by two different institutions. Historic merit is settled by historiography and the archive: the object as evidence, as hinge, as the thing after which the field was not the same. Artistic merit is settled by criticism and the aesthetic field: the object as achievement, as a made thing that continues to reward attention. The category error in the "versus" is to imagine a scale on which the two are weighed against each other. They are weighed on different scales, in different places, by different people. A work can be heavy on one and weightless on the other, and no exchange rate converts between them.

## What canonization actually measures

The dispute over what canonization measures is the field's oldest, and neither pole of it survives the encounter with the material. On one side, Harold Bloom holds that the canon records aesthetic strength, meaning originality, a certain "strangeness," the agonistic overcoming of predecessors, and that its authority is aesthetic rather than social.[@bloom1994] On the other stands the institutional account. John Guillory reframes canon formation not as a referendum on intrinsic worth but as a problem in the distribution of cultural capital, transacted above all through the school syllabus.[@guillory1993] Pierre Bourdieu locates value in the field itself: a work "exists as a symbolic object only if it is known and recognized," and consecration is performed by intermediaries (critics, juries, curators) who hold the authority to confer it.[@bourdieu1993] Barbara Herrnstein Smith presses hardest, arguing that the very endurance we take as proof of a classic's intrinsic worth is an _effect_ of institutional transmission rather than its cause: the work is continually re-produced as valuable by the systems that keep teaching, printing, and screening it.[@smith1988] James English supplies the machinery in close-up, showing that prizes and awards are engines converting economic capital into cultural capital and back, and that the Academy Award is among the most efficient consecration devices modern culture has built.[@english2005]

The temptation is to side with the institutionalists and be done. But Bloom cannot be dismissed so cleanly, because the constructivist account carries its own unpaid debt. Smith can explain why heavily transmitted works stay in circulation; she cannot explain why some of them are nonetheless abandoned, why audiences walk away from consecrated objects, why a merit-claim, once made, can be refused. If value were only conferred, refusal would be impossible. The truce worth proposing is that merit is neither purely intrinsic nor purely granted. It is a _relation_ between a made object and an institution equipped to register a particular kind of value in it. This is why there is not one canon but as many canons as there are institutions doing the registering, and why the singular "the canon" is a convenient fiction that hides the plurality doing the work.

## Canon and archive

Aleida Assmann's distinction between canon and archive sharpens the point.[@assmann2008] She separates cultural memory into two modes. The _canon_ is the small body of works kept actively in circulation, taught and performed and quoted, kept warm by continual use. The _archive_ is the vast latent store of what is retained but not circulated, catalogued and available but dormant. Mapped onto the two merits, the alignment is almost exact. Historic merit earns a work the archive: it is preserved because it mattered, because the record would be incomplete without it. Artistic merit earns a work the canon: it is kept warm because it still repays the attention.[^site]

## The distinction across media

The two merits come apart most visibly when they are laid across different media, where the consecrating institutions are of different ages and their verdicts do not yet pretend to agree.

**Film.** Two of the decade's largest films show the split at its cleanest. _Top Gun: Maverick_ (2022) is a study in artistic merit within total convention. Its beats are time-worn (a fall from grace, an impossible assignment, a training montage, a redemption), every needle-drop and crossfade a quotation of 1986, and yet it is executed so well that it drew audiences back to theaters after the pandemic and took six Academy nominations, Best Picture among them, and winning Best Sound. It carries almost no historic novelty; its merit is entirely in the doing, and its consecration ran through the box office and the prize economy into the sub-canon of the legacy sequel. _Avengers: Endgame_ (2019) is the mirror image. From July 2019 until _Avatar_'s re-release reclaimed the record in March 2021, it was the highest-grossing film ever made; it is also the first film to hold that title and win zero Oscars, taking a single nomination, for Visual Effects, and losing it. _Endgame_ is a historic object, a hinge in a decade-long superhero cycle and an accelerant of the fatigue that followed, consecrated overwhelmingly by scale and almost not at all by criticism. _Maverick_ has artistic merit the archive will forget; _Endgame_ has historic merit the aesthetic canon will decline. Neither occupies a middle ground between cult and canon. Each is fully canonized, in a different canon.

**Games.** The same split recurs where the institutions are younger and the stakes are plainer. _Myst_ (1993) was for years the best-selling personal-computer game ever made and did as much as any single title to drive the adoption of the CD-ROM; its historic merit is beyond dispute and its artistic reputation has never stopped being argued.[^myst] _Disco Elysium_ (2019) sold a fraction as many copies but was consecrated almost at once by critics as a formal achievement, a role-playing game that made prose and moral reasoning its mechanics rather than its decoration. One entered the archive of the medium's history; the other entered its aesthetic canon; the distinction the "versus" could not hold is here simply two different doors, clearly marked.

**Music.** A further axis, that cult and canon are not sequential, becomes legible in the record most often cited for it. _The Velvet Underground & Nico_ (1967) sold poorly and was, in its moment, a commercial failure and a cult possession; the line often attributed to Brian Eno holds that only a few thousand people bought it, but every one of them started a band.[^eno] Its later canonization did not erase its cult; the two coexist, the record at once a consecrated monument and a subcultural password. Canon and cult, it turns out, were never points on a single line.

**The internet.** The cleanest contemporary case is the one this project took as its emblem. The _Backrooms_ began in 2022 as a teenager's analog-horror videos on YouTube, the purest cult object, native to a platform the aesthetic institutions had no grammar for. In May 2026 it arrived in theaters as an A24 feature directed by that same teenager, Kane Parsons, produced for roughly ten million dollars and grossing more than three hundred million, the highest-grossing film in the studio's history.[@backrooms2026] The pipeline the site is named after ran, visibly, from start to finish, in under four years. But notice what was consecrated and what was not. The box office and the studio's imprimatur are a historic and commercial consecration; whether the film has _artistic_ merit is a separate adjudication, conducted in a different room, by critics working out in real time whether they possess a vocabulary adequate to an object that came up through channels their canon did not build. The interesting question is not whether the _Backrooms_ made it, since commercially it plainly did, but whether its two consecrations will converge or stay apart, as they did for _Endgame_.

## What consecration does to the object

A harder question follows: once something is consecrated, what does consecration _do_ to it? Does it seal the work in a box to be observed from outside, or leave it alive to use? Walter Benjamin's terms predate fandom but fit it uncannily.[@benjamin1936] He distinguishes a work's _cult value_, its worth in ritual and presence and use, from its _exhibition value_, its worth as a thing displayed and appraised.[^benjamin-cult] Canonization tends to convert the first into the second: it lifts the object onto the wall, where it gains exhibition value and risks losing the warmth of use. That conversion is the sealed box, the museum piece observed from outside and no longer handled. But it is not the only fate, and here Umberto Eco is the corrective. In his account of the cult text, a work becomes cultic not by being perfect but by being "ramshackle," a "completely furnished world" whose devotees can dismantle it into quotable parts and reinhabit them.[@eco1985] Henry Jenkins names the practice: fans as poachers who occupy a text they do not own, producing headcanon, theory, and continuation.[@jenkins1992] A consecrated work can sustain exactly this. The _Star Wars_ and Marvel canons are, at the same moment, institutionally consecrated properties and the furnished worlds of an enormous poaching culture. Canon and cult, exhibition value and cult value, are not stages on a line but coordinates on a plane; a given work occupies some position on both at once.

## Conclusion

The "versus" is the error. The intuition that historic significance alone does not make a work _artistically_ canonical is sound; it simply mistakes one canon for the whole institution of canonicity. There is no single gate and no gatekeeper with universal writ. There are archives that keep what mattered and canons that keep what still rewards attention; there are prize juries, syllabi, box-office charts, critics' polls, and subcultures, each consecrating the kind of value it is built to measure, and each producing its own apocrypha in the same gesture that produces its canon. To ask whether a work "deserves the canon" is to ask a question with no addressee. The answerable questions are the ones this project exists to work: who is consecrating this object, by what authority, into which canon, measuring what, and what the object becomes once they have. Canonization is a process, not a verdict, and the writer, choosing which merit to credit and which to withhold, is one of its operators.
