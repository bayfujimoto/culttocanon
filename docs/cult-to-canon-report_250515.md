# Cult to Canon: A Consolidated Report

## Part One — On the Phrase and Its Stakes

The substitution of "canon" for "classic" is not a minor lexical adjustment; it transforms the conceptual stakes of the project entirely. Where "classic" denotes a vague honorific—a work of acknowledged quality, a status conferred by consensus and time—"canon" invokes a far more freighted apparatus. The canon is not merely a list of esteemed works but an institution, a mechanism of selection and exclusion, a site of contestation. To speak of canonization is to speak of power: who decides, by what criteria, with what consequences for what is remembered and what is permitted to vanish. Your phrase therefore names not a gentle ascent but a process of legitimation, and the project it describes assumes a sharper polemical edge.

The word "canon" carries a theological inheritance that should not be ignored. It derives from the Greek *kanōn*, meaning a measuring rod or rule, and entered cultural discourse via the early Church's process of determining which texts constituted scripture and which were apocryphal. This etymology is illuminating: a canon is not a description of what exists but a prescription for what counts. The corresponding act of *canonization* is therefore always also an act of *de-canonization*—the apocryphal is produced by the same gesture that produces the sacred.

To move from "cult" to "canon" is therefore to traverse a charged threshold. The cult object exists in a state that scholars of religion such as Mircea Eliade and, more recently, scholars of fandom such as Henry Jenkins (*Textual Poachers*, 1992) have characterized as one of intimate, participatory devotion. Canonization disrupts this intimacy. The work becomes property of the institution—the academy, the criticism establishment, the streaming service's "essentials" list—and the original community of devotees often experiences this elevation as a kind of loss. Pierre Bourdieu's *The Rules of Art* (1992) and *Distinction* (1979) provide the indispensable theoretical apparatus here: canonization is the conversion of subcultural capital into legitimate cultural capital, and this conversion always involves a transfer of authority from the original community to the institutions that subsequently consecrate the work.

As Frank Kermode argues in *The Classic* (1975) and elaborates in *Forms of Attention* (1985), canons are not discovered but made—and the making is always a contest between the institutional and the heretical, the preservers and the rediscoverers. Your site is positioned to interrogate this transfer. It could trace specific instances of it, theorize it broadly, resist it polemically, or perform it deliberately. Each posture yields a different project.

## Part Two — Reflections on the Initial Ideas

**The TUI aesthetic and visual minimalism.** The terminal interface acquires significance under the rubric of canon. The TUI is itself a kind of countercanonical gesture—an insistence on textual primacy in a visual economy, on slowness in a culture of acceleration, on the readable over the merely scannable. Olia Lialina's "A Vernacular Web" (2005) and the broader Net Art tradition theorize such interface choices as critical interventions rather than mere aesthetic preferences. Mark Fisher's *Ghosts of My Life* (2014) develops the related concept of hauntology, in which obsolete forms carry the residue of futures that never arrived. A TUI in 2026 is therefore neither nostalgic nor neutral; it is, to invoke Svetlana Boym's distinction in *The Future of Nostalgia* (2001), reflective rather than restorative—it acknowledges historical distance rather than attempting to abolish it. The choice between ASCII and Ben Day dots merits further thought: ASCII belongs to the genealogy of the textual and the early computational, while the Ben Day dot belongs to mechanical reproduction and the Pop Art interrogation of mass culture (Lichtenstein, but also the broader theoretical apparatus of Walter Benjamin's "The Work of Art in the Age of Mechanical Reproduction"). These genealogies are not interchangeable, and selecting between them is itself a canonical decision in miniature.

**Watching obscure films and YouTube features.** Under the framework of canon, this practice becomes something more pointed than cinephilic exploration. It becomes an act of what Jacques Rancière, in *The Politics of Aesthetics* (2000), calls a redistribution of the sensible—a redrawing of the line between what is considered legitimate object of serious attention and what is not. The recent flourishing of long-form YouTube horror—Kane Pixels, the Backrooms cycle, analog horror as an emergent genre, the SCP Foundation's transmedia mythology—constitutes precisely the kind of material that canon-formation typically excludes and that countercanonical projects exist to recover. Adam Charles Hart's *Monstrous Forms: Moving Image Horror Across Media* (Oxford, 2020) and the journal *Horror Studies* provide useful scholarly precedent. Your site could function as a kind of premature canonization—taking seriously, in advance of institutional recognition, works that the apparatus has not yet noticed.

**Improving your writing.** Canon-formation has historically been a writerly act. The critics who matter—Sainte-Beuve, Eliot, Leavis, Trilling, Kael, Sontag—did not merely report on existing consensus but actively constructed taste through prose. T.S. Eliot's "Tradition and the Individual Talent" (1919) remains the foundational meditation on how new works retroactively reorganize the canon they enter. To write seriously about marginal works is therefore not separate from canonical politics but constitutive of them.

**Uncertainty of purpose.** The framework of canon helpfully constrains without prescribing. Whatever the site becomes—blog, database, review repository, fiction archive—its organizing tension is now legible: it concerns the contested boundary between the marginal and the legitimate, the apocryphal and the scriptural.

## Part Three — Adjacent Branches Worth Investigating

**The literature of the canon wars and their aftermath.** The 1980s and 1990s saw intense academic debate over canon-formation, with figures such as Harold Bloom (*The Western Canon*, 1994) defending traditional canons and a generation of critics—John Guillory's *Cultural Capital* (1993) is the most theoretically rigorous example—analyzing canon-formation as institutional practice. This literature provides the vocabulary for understanding what canonization actually is as a social process.

**Fan studies and the sociology of cult reception.** Henry Jenkins' *Textual Poachers* (1992), Matt Hills' *Fan Cultures* (2002), and the journal *Transformative Works and Cultures* document how cult communities form around marginal works and how they relate—often ambivalently—to subsequent institutional recognition.

**Paracinema and the politics of bad taste.** Jeffrey Sconce's "'Trashing' the Academy: Taste, Excess, and an Emerging Politics of Cinematic Style" (*Screen*, 1995) introduced "paracinema" as a term for the deliberate cultivation of marginal, degraded, or low films as a form of cultural critique. Joan Hawkins' *Cutting Edge: Art-Horror and the Horrific Avant-Garde* (2000) extends this analysis.

**The database as canonical form.** Lev Manovich's *The Language of New Media* (2001) argues that the database has displaced narrative as the dominant cultural logic of our moment. Canons themselves are databases, and the construction of a database—its categories, its relations, its inclusions and exclusions—is therefore always a canonical act. Borges' "The Library of Babel" and his essay "The Analytical Language of John Wilkins" (which Foucault famously invokes in the preface to *The Order of Things*) demonstrate that taxonomic structures are never innocent.

**The apocrypha.** The companion to canon is always the apocryphal—the texts excluded, the works deemed unworthy, the readings rejected. Scholarship on apocryphal traditions suggests that the excluded materials often illuminate the canonical ones more sharply than the canonical materials illuminate themselves. A site organized explicitly around the apocryphal would have considerable conceptual coherence.

## Part Four — A Brainstorm of Possible Forms

### Possible Purposes

- **The Premature Canonization Project.** Take seriously, in advance of institutional recognition, works and figures and ideas that the legitimating apparatus has not yet noticed. Need not be cinematic—could include obscure essayists, minor philosophers, forgotten novels, abandoned subcultures, dead websites, internet folklore.
- **The Apocrypha Archive.** Dwell in the marginal as marginal. Celebrate what cannot be canonized without distortion.
- **The Field Notes of a Reader.** A record of sustained attention, in the lineage of Montaigne's *Essais* or Geoff Dyer's deliberately unsystematic critical work.
- **The Counter-Canon.** Construct, over time, an alternative canon. Unapologetically prescriptive.
- **The Theory of Canonization.** Meta rather than primary. Examine the mechanisms by which things become canonical.
- **The Slow Web Outpost.** Exist as a deliberate counterweight to platform logic and attention economies.
- **The Writing Gymnasium.** The site exists primarily for development as a writer and thinker.
- **The Polemical Journal.** Each piece is constructed as a sustained argument with a thesis, opposing views, and a defended conclusion.

### Possible Organizations

- **The Taxonomic Site.** Content rigorously categorized; the taxonomy itself becomes a creative work, as in Borges or Perec.
- **The Anti-Taxonomic Site.** No categories, no tags, no archive page. The refusal to organize is the gesture.
- **The Constellation.** Pieces link to other pieces by thematic resonance rather than by date or category.
- **The Garden.** Pieces as living notes that grow, get revised, get linked. Nothing is ever quite done.
- **The Calendar.** Organized strictly by time. The constraint produces the discipline.
- **The Sequence.** A single ongoing project with a clear arc.
- **The Notebook.** The site is openly a working document. Drafts visible, revisions tracked.
- **The Library.** Each piece a discrete artifact; the site as idiosyncratic private collection.

### Possible Stances

- **The Earnest Critic.** Straightforward seriousness; no irony, no hedging.
- **The Ironist.** Intelligent detachment, in the mode of Sontag's late essays.
- **The Enthusiast.** Open advocacy and undisguised love—the Kael or Marcus model.
- **The Skeptic.** Polemical, critique-driven—Hazlitt, Macdonald.
- **The Pseudonymous Voice.** Write under a constructed persona to sharpen rhetorical instincts.
- **The Anonymous Voice.** No authorial identity; the work stands alone.
- **The Conversational Voice.** Write as though speaking to a particular reader.

### Possible Functions

- **Long-form essay.** Sustained reasoning, one to five thousand words.
- **The fragment.** Aphoristic precision in the lineage of Lichtenberg, Cioran, Adorno.
- **The review.** Close reading and judgment.
- **The annotated bibliography.** Concision and characterization.
- **Original fiction.** Narrative, voice, structure.
- **Dialogue.** Dramatize argument; inhabit positions other than your own.
- **The lecture.** Pieces written to be spoken—directly relevant to speaking goals.
- **The case study.** Research and synthesis on a single phenomenon.
- **The translation or commentary.** Close engagement with prose other than your own.
- **The list.** As serious form, in the manner of Sontag's "Notes on Camp."

### Possible Registers

- **The academic register.** Footnoted, citation-heavy, formal. Develops rigor.
- **The belletristic register.** Polished, allusive, prose-as-pleasure. Develops voice.
- **The plainspoken register.** Stripped down, in the lineage of Orwell. Develops clarity.
- **The hybrid register.** Deliberate mixing of high and low. Develops range.
- **The performative register.** Self-aware, recursive, occasionally metafictional. Develops sophistication about form.

A useful discipline: write a single piece, then rewrite it in each register. The exercise reveals which is genuinely yours and which are costumes.

### Provocations

What if the site had no homepage—only entry points through specific pieces? What if each piece appeared in a different visual treatment? What if the site published one piece per month and then took it down a month later, refusing archival permanence? What if the site existed only as a feed with no archive? What if every piece was constrained—fixed word count, forbidden vocabulary, required structure—in the manner of Oulipo? What if the site openly tracked its own development, showing drafts and revisions and abandoned pieces?

## Part Five — On the Container

You have arrived at the central problem of experimental practice: how to build a vessel capacious enough to hold genuinely varied work without dissolving into mere miscellany. You have already named the two things that hold the site together—the organizing phrase and the singular authorial consciousness. These are not minor binding agents; they are the two most durable forms of unity that experimental work has historically relied upon.

### The Authorial Mind as Container

The oldest container for heterogeneous work is the unity of a single sensibility. Montaigne's *Essais*, begun in 1572, range across cannibals, thumbs, friendship, sleep, the education of children, and death; what holds them together is Montaigne himself. He named the form *essai*, an attempt, precisely to license this range. The reader comes not for a topic but for a mind.

This model has been renewed repeatedly. Walter Benjamin's *One-Way Street* (1928) collects aphorisms, dream descriptions, urban observations, and theoretical fragments under a single organizing consciousness. Roland Barthes' *Roland Barthes by Roland Barthes* (1975) is a fragmented self-portrait whose unity is its refusal of conventional autobiographical unity. Maggie Nelson (*Bluets*, *The Argonauts*) and Brian Dillon (*Essayism*, *Suppose a Sentence*) demonstrate that authorial consciousness remains a viable container in the present.

You do not need a topic. You need a *voice*—a recognizable set of preoccupations, rhythms, intellectual habits, and aesthetic commitments. The voice need not be present in every piece, but the underlying sensibility will hold the disparate work together.

### The Conceptual Frame

Sol LeWitt's "Paragraphs on Conceptual Art" (1967) argued that the idea is the most important aspect of the work, and that executions can vary while the concept holds. Applied to a website, "cult to canon" need not be the subject of every piece but the conceptual operation by which the site as a whole functions.

Among websites, Maria Popova's *The Marginalian* demonstrates a stable conceptual frame holding together extremely varied content. Robin Sloan's and Craig Mod's sites operate similarly. For "cult to canon," the conceptual frame is unusually strong—it names a process, a politics, and a posture all at once.

### The Container That Shows Its Seams

The relevant precedent is the *commonplace book*, a form that flourished from the Renaissance through the nineteenth century. Bacon, Milton, Locke, Coleridge, Emerson, and Auden kept commonplace books—personal repositories for quotations, observations, fragments. Auden's *A Certain World* (1970) was published as an explicit commonplace book.

In the digital era, this lineage continues in *digital gardening*, articulated by Mike Caulfield in "The Garden and the Stream: A Technopastoral" (2015) and developed by Maggie Appleton, Andy Matuschak, and Tom Critchlow. Where the blog is chronological, performative, and finished, the garden is associative, exploratory, and perpetually under cultivation. Pages are dated by both creation and last revision and marked with maturity indicators—seedling, budding, evergreen.

Andy Matuschak's site (andymatuschak.org) is canonical. His public notes are interlinked, varying in length and finish, marked with confidence levels, openly provisional. Tom Critchlow's site uses "wiki-style" pages alongside formal essays. Gwern Branwen's site (gwern.net) takes the form to an extreme, with every page bearing metadata about confidence, completion, and revision history.

You can publish drafts and finished pieces in the same space, marked clearly as to their status. You can show revision history. You can maintain pages of fragments and notes-toward-larger-pieces alongside polished essays. You can openly preserve abandoned drafts. The TUI aesthetic is unusually well-suited to this approach, because the terminal interface naturally accommodates metadata, status flags, and version information without aesthetic friction. A piece could be marked `[draft v.3]` or `[abandoned 2026-03]` or `[evergreen]` in plain text.

### The Constellation Model

A different binding strategy treats the site as constellation—points connected by lines of association rather than organized into hierarchies. The theoretical lineage runs through Ted Nelson's *Computer Lib/Dream Machines* (1974), through Michael Joyce's *afternoon, a story* (1987) and Shelley Jackson's *Patchwork Girl* (1995), through the present-day Zettelkasten note-taking movement.

A piece on Borges might link to a piece on canonical authority, to a fragment about libraries, to a short fiction about an imaginary book, and to an annotated bibliography on classification. The reader experiences the site as a network of mutual illumination rather than as a sorted archive.

Robin Sloan's "stock and flow" distinction is useful here. Durable online work combines *flow*—the regular, perishable stream that maintains presence—with *stock*—the more permanent, deeper work that accumulates over time. Your stream of new fragments and essays is the flow; the gradually thickening web of interlinked deeper pieces is the stock.

### The Sequence Within the Container

You might maintain a numbered series of fragments alongside a series of monthly essays, alongside a serial fiction, alongside one-off reviews. Each series has its own conventions and rhythms. *The Whole Earth Catalog* held essays, reviews, lists, and fragments under Stewart Brand's editorial sensibility. *Cabinet* magazine places scholarly essays beside artist projects beside oddities beside fictions, held together only by a shared register of intellectual curiosity.

The structure might run roughly as follows: the site contains a stream of dated fragments (the flow); a growing archive of longer essays, fictions, dialogues, and polemics (the stock), each marked with status; one or more ongoing serial projects with their own internal logic; and a network of links connecting resonant pieces across categories. The unifying frame is "cult to canon" as conceptual operation; the unifying voice is yours; the unifying form is the TUI; the unifying honesty is the willingness to show the work in progress.

### Practical Considerations

The interface should make status legible. Simple textual indicators—`[draft]`, `[revision 4]`, `[abandoned]`, `[evergreen]`—work better than visual ones in a TUI context and are part of the site's argument.

Dating matters more in a gardened site than in a blog. Each piece benefits from both a creation date and a most-recent-revision date. The visible accumulation of revisions over time is part of what the site is documenting.

The homepage problem deserves thought. A site this varied resists summary. Solutions include the recent-activity stream, the deliberately partial homepage that trusts the reader to wander, or the rotating entry point.

An archive page remains valuable even when taxonomic organization is resisted—a single dense page that the curious can reach.

Whether to permit external attention—comments, social sharing buttons, analytics—is itself a meaningful design decision. Your lack of monetization concerns gives you freedom to refuse them entirely.

## Part Six — Concluding Reflection

The strongest version of "cult to canon" will not merely describe the migration its title names but will *intervene* in it. To take seriously, in the considered register of essayistic prose and the disciplined frame of a deliberately constructed interface, works that the institutional apparatus has not yet noticed—this is not commentary on canonization but its enactment. Each piece of sustained writing about an analog horror YouTube series, each careful taxonomy of obscure films, each fragment of original fiction that takes the marginal as serious material, performs the elevation the title proposes.

The combination you are describing—a TUI-aesthetic, text-primary site that holds essays, fragments, fictions, reviews, drafts, and abandoned pieces under the conceptual frame of "cult to canon" and the unifying sensibility of a single author—is not without precedent, but its specific configuration would be genuinely your own. The closest analogues are Matuschak, Gwern, Critchlow, and the broader digital gardening community, but those sites are largely concerned with knowledge management. Yours would inflect the form toward criticism, fiction, and cultural argument, which is a relatively underexplored register for the digital garden.

The most important practical advice: begin before the container is fully specified. The structure will emerge from the work, not the other way around. Build a minimal version with one essay, one fragment, one draft, and one abandoned piece, and let the design questions arise from the experience of holding those specific objects together. Christopher Alexander, in *A Pattern Language* (1977) and *The Timeless Way of Building*, argued that genuine form emerges from the iterated solution of specific problems rather than from the imposition of a prior plan. The same is true of websites.

What you can know in advance is that the two binding agents you have already identified—the phrase and the singular author—are sufficient. Trust them. The rest is iteration.
