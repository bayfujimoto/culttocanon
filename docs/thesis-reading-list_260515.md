# Reading List for the Site Thesis

A curated bibliography to ground the writing of a site thesis defining *cult*, *canon*, and the movement between them. Organized by line of inquiry rather than alphabetically — read across sections, not within them.

---

## 1. Etymology and origin

Before defining the terms for the site, define them for yourself by looking at where they came from.

- **Bruce Metzger**, *The Canon of the New Testament: Its Origin, Development, and Significance* (Oxford, 1987). The standard scholarly account of how the word *canon* (Greek *kanōn*, "rule" or "measuring rod") came to mean a closed list of authoritative texts. The religious origin is not incidental; it shapes every secular use.
- **Jonathan Z. Smith**, *Imagining Religion: From Babylon to Jonestown* (Chicago, 1982), especially "Sacred Persistence: Toward a Redescription of Canon." Smith argues canon is a *generative* category — what gets ruled in compels endless exegesis of what is ruled out.
- **Walter Burkert**, *Greek Religion* (Harvard, 1985). For the parallel etymology of *cult* (Latin *cultus*, "care, cultivation, worship"). Note that *cult* and *culture* share a root — both are forms of tending.
- **Raymond Williams**, *Keywords: A Vocabulary of Culture and Society* (1976), entries on "canon," "culture," "tradition," "taste." Indispensable for stripping the words back to their working senses.

## 2. Canon-formation as historical practice — across cultures

The thesis is incomplete without breadth. Western canon-theory has often pretended to universality; the corrective is to read traditions that built their own canons on different grounds.

### Greek and Roman

- **Aristotle**, *Poetics*. The earliest surviving systematic canon-theory in the West — what tragedy is, which poets did it well, why.
- **Quintilian**, *Institutio Oratoria* X.1 (1st c. CE). A working reading list for Roman rhetorical education — canon-formation in operational form.
- **Reynolds & Wilson**, *Scribes and Scholars: A Guide to the Transmission of Greek and Latin Literature* (Oxford, 4th ed. 2013). How antiquity reached us at all — the material history of canon survival.

### Chinese

- **Confucius**, *Analects*. The figure who, more than any other, defines what canonization meant in East Asia. The *Five Classics* (五經) are the result.
- **Liu Xie** (劉勰), *Wenxin diaolong* (文心雕龍, "The Literary Mind and the Carving of Dragons"), c. 501 CE. The most ambitious work of premodern Chinese literary theory; treats genre, style, and value systematically.
- **Zhong Rong** (鍾嶸), *Shipin* (詩品, "Grades of Poetry"), c. 513 CE. Explicit ranking of 122 poets into three tiers — a canon-formation apparatus in pure form, centuries before the West had anything comparable.
- **Stephen Owen**, *Readings in Chinese Literary Thought* (Harvard, 1992). The best English-language entry point.

### Sanskrit

- **Bharata**, *Nāṭyaśāstra* (c. 200 BCE – 200 CE). The foundational text of Indian aesthetics; introduces *rasa* theory.
- **Ānandavardhana**, *Dhvanyāloka* ("Light on Suggestion"), 9th c., with **Abhinavagupta**'s commentary. Argues poetic value resides in *dhvani*, suggestion — a non-Western theory of literary worth radically unlike Western mimesis-based accounts.
- **Sheldon Pollock**, *The Language of the Gods in the World of Men* (California, 2006), and his anthology *A Rasa Reader* (Columbia, 2016).

### Arabic and Persian

- **Al-Jāḥiẓ**, *Kitāb al-Bayān wa-l-Tabyīn* ("The Book of Eloquence and Demonstration"), 9th c. Foundational treatment of the Arabic *adab* tradition — a canon of style and conduct, not just texts.
- **Ibn Khaldūn**, *Muqaddimah* (1377). The most ambitious premodern theory of how cultures produce and lose their canons, embedded in a theory of civilizational rise and fall.

### Japanese

- **Murasaki Shikibu**, *Tale of Genji* (early 11th c.), and the body of criticism around it from Motoori Norinaga (*Genji Monogatari Tama no Ogushi*, 1799) to the present — a case study in how a single work gets canonized inside its culture and then exported.
- **Haruo Shirane**, *Traces of Dreams: Landscape, Cultural Memory, and the Poetry of Bashō* (Stanford, 1998). On the formation of the haikai canon.

### Religious canon-formation as comparative laboratory

- **The Pali Canon (Tipiṭaka)** — its councils and closure offer a textbook case of how a community decides what is and is not authoritative.
- **The hadith sciences** — al-Bukhārī and Muslim ibn al-Ḥajjāj's compilations (9th c.) as the most rigorous canon-formation method ever invented, with explicit *isnād* (chain-of-transmission) criteria for inclusion.
- **Jonathan Z. Smith** (see §1) is the bridge between religious and literary canon theory.

## 3. Theories of taste and aesthetic judgment

The philosophical substrate. The question "what makes something worth attending to?" predates the canon wars by centuries.

- **David Hume**, "Of the Standard of Taste" (1757). The starting point for any modern theory of judgment.
- **Immanuel Kant**, *Critique of the Power of Judgment* (1790), §§1–22. Demanding, but the source of the "disinterested" judgment everything later either inherits or rejects.
- **Friedrich Schiller**, *On the Aesthetic Education of Man* (1795). The case for aesthetic experience as a constitutive human capacity.
- **G.W.F. Hegel**, *Lectures on Aesthetics* (delivered 1820s). The first major attempt to historicize taste — to argue that what counts as beautiful changes with epoch.
- **Walter Pater**, *The Renaissance: Studies in Art and Poetry* (1873). The aesthete's case; the "hard, gemlike flame" passage in the conclusion is the manifesto of cult-attention avant la lettre.
- **Friedrich Schlegel**, *Athenaeum Fragments* (1798). The Romantic theory of the fragment as a complete form — directly relevant to your `kind: fragment` field.

## 4. The Anglo-American canon tradition

The lineage you are working both with and against.

- **Matthew Arnold**, *Culture and Anarchy* (1869) and "The Function of Criticism at the Present Time" (1864). The case for criticism as a civilizing apparatus.
- **T.S. Eliot**, "Tradition and the Individual Talent" (1919). The single most cited essay on canon-formation in English. Read it; then read the case against it.
- **F.R. Leavis**, *The Great Tradition* (Chatto & Windus, 1948). The most famous (and most attacked) English-language canon-statement of the 20th c.
- **Frank Kermode**, *The Classic* (1975) and *The Genesis of Secrecy* (1979). The subtlest English critic on what makes a text endure.
- **Erich Auerbach**, *Mimesis: The Representation of Reality in Western Literature* (Princeton, 1953; German 1946). A single book that is itself a canon-formation act, written from exile in Istanbul without access to a research library — the conditions of its composition are part of its meaning.
- **Ernst Robert Curtius**, *European Literature and the Latin Middle Ages* (Princeton, 1953; German 1948). The companion volume to Auerbach.
- **Harold Bloom**, *The Western Canon: The Books and School of the Ages* (Harcourt Brace, 1994). The defense of a closed Western canon. Read it as a primary document of the position you will probably have to argue against, in whole or in part.
- **Italo Calvino**, *Why Read the Classics?* (Pantheon, 1999; Italian 1991). Short, lucid, indispensable. The fourteen definitions of "a classic" in the title essay are themselves a working thesis.

## 5. Sociology of cultural production

Where canon-formation becomes a research object rather than an article of faith.

- **Pierre Bourdieu**, *Distinction: A Social Critique of the Judgement of Taste* (Harvard, 1984; French 1979); *The Rules of Art* (Stanford, 1996; French 1992); "The Field of Cultural Production" (1983, collected in the 1993 volume). The foundational sociological theory. Once you have read Bourdieu you cannot read criticism naively again.
- **James English**, *The Economy of Prestige: Prizes, Awards, and the Circulation of Cultural Value* (Harvard, 2005). The best book on how cultural authority is produced and traded.
- **Pascale Casanova**, *The World Republic of Letters* (Harvard, 2004; French 1999). World-systems theory applied to literary canon — which capitals consecrate what.
- **Mark McGurl**, *The Program Era: Postwar Fiction and the Rise of Creative Writing* (Harvard, 2009). How the MFA system reshaped the American novelistic canon. McGurl's *Everything and Less* (Verso, 2021) extends the thesis to Amazon.
- **Lawrence Levine**, *Highbrow/Lowbrow: The Emergence of Cultural Hierarchy in America* (Harvard, 1988). The history of how Shakespeare went from popular to "high" in 19th-c. America — a perfect case study in canon-movement.
- **John Carey**, *The Intellectuals and the Masses: Pride and Prejudice Among the Literary Intelligentsia, 1880–1939* (Faber, 1992). The polemical counter-history.

## 6. The canon wars and decolonial critique

Still unresolved; central to defining what canon means in 2026.

- **John Guillory**, *Cultural Capital: The Problem of Literary Canon Formation* (Chicago, 1993). The most rigorous internal critique of canon-thinking. Required.
- **Henry Louis Gates Jr.**, *Loose Canons: Notes on the Culture Wars* (Oxford, 1992).
- **Toni Morrison**, *Playing in the Dark: Whiteness and the Literary Imagination* (Harvard, 1992). What an Africanist presence does to the American canon when you finally see it.
- **Edward Said**, *Orientalism* (Pantheon, 1978); *Culture and Imperialism* (Knopf, 1993).
- **Gayatri Chakravorty Spivak**, "Can the Subaltern Speak?" (1988); *Death of a Discipline* (Columbia, 2003).
- **Chinua Achebe**, "An Image of Africa: Racism in Conrad's *Heart of Darkness*" (*Massachusetts Review*, 1977). One of the most consequential single canon-interventions ever written.
- **Ngũgĩ wa Thiong'o**, *Decolonising the Mind* (Heinemann, 1986).
- **Édouard Glissant**, *Poetics of Relation* (Michigan, 1997; French 1990). The most generative theoretical alternative to canon-as-closed-list.

## 7. Cult, subculture, fandom, camp — the other half of the title

The thesis cannot define *canon* without defining *cult*, and the literature here is its own tradition.

- **Susan Sontag**, "Notes on 'Camp'" (*Partisan Review*, 1964); *Against Interpretation* (1966); "On Style" (1965). Sontag is, in many ways, the bridge — a critic who took cult attention seriously without abandoning canonical seriousness.
- **Dick Hebdige**, *Subculture: The Meaning of Style* (Routledge, 1979). The foundational subculture-theory text.
- **Umberto Eco**, "Casablanca: Cult Movies and Intertextual Collage" (1985, in *Travels in Hyperreality*). The essay that names what makes a cult object cultic.
- **Henry Jenkins**, *Textual Poachers: Television Fans and Participatory Culture* (Routledge, 1992). Fandom as canon-formation from below.
- **J. Hoberman and Jonathan Rosenbaum**, *Midnight Movies* (Harper & Row, 1983). The canonical history of cult cinema, by two of its best critics.
- **Mark Fisher**, *Capitalist Realism* (Zero, 2009); *Ghosts of My Life* (Zero, 2014); *The Weird and the Eerie* (Repeater, 2016). Fisher is the single most useful living-recent influence for what you are trying to do.
- **Greil Marcus**, *Lipstick Traces: A Secret History of the Twentieth Century* (Harvard, 1989). Punk and Dada read together — a model of how to write the cult-canon hybrid essay.
- **Matt Hills**, *Fan Cultures* (Routledge, 2002); **Ernest Mathijs and Jamie Sexton**, *Cult Cinema: An Introduction* (Wiley-Blackwell, 2011). The academic field as it currently stands.

## 8. Paratext, marginalia, glosses, footnotes

Foundational for the site's Marginalia pane and for any theory of how secondary writing becomes primary.

- **Gérard Genette**, *Paratexts: Thresholds of Interpretation* (Cambridge, 1997; French 1987). The book the Marginalia pane is, in effect, a digital realization of.
- **H.J. Jackson**, *Marginalia: Readers Writing in Books* (Yale, 2001). On annotation as a form of authorship.
- **William H. Sherman**, *Used Books: Marking Readers in Renaissance England* (Penn, 2008). Marginalia as historical practice.
- **Anthony Grafton**, *The Footnote: A Curious History* (Harvard, 1997). The footnote as a tiny canon-formation engine.

## 9. The digital and contemporary moment

What canon-formation looks like when the apparatus is algorithmic, networked, and partially anonymous.

- **Hito Steyerl**, *The Wretched of the Screen* (Sternberg, 2012); "In Defense of the Poor Image" (*e-flux*, 2009). The best theorist of the image under digital conditions.
- **Lev Manovich**, *The Language of New Media* (MIT, 2001). Now somewhat dated, but the language it gives you remains usable.
- **Andy Matuschak**, "Evergreen notes" and the related essays at andymatuschak.org. The clearest current articulation of how to cultivate a personal knowledge-canon.
- **Gwern Branwen**, gwern.net, especially the essays on long-form writing and the use of the `confidence` and `importance` tags. The vocabulary your schema already borrows.
- **Sianne Ngai**, *Our Aesthetic Categories: Zany, Cute, Interesting* (Harvard, 2012); *Theory of the Gimmick* (Harvard, 2020). The most rigorous current theorist of how new aesthetic categories emerge.

## 10. Working critics as models of practice

Not all theory. These are the people whose *essays themselves* model the kind of canon-formation work the site is for. Read them as much for the prose as for the argument.

- **Susan Sontag** (already cited, but: *Where the Stress Falls*, 2001).
- **Geoffrey O'Brien**, *The Phantom Empire* (Norton, 1993); *Stolen Glimpses, Captive Shadows* (Counterpoint, 2013).
- **Pauline Kael**, *5001 Nights at the Movies* (Holt, 1982). The capsule review as canon-formation in miniature.
- **John Berger**, *Ways of Seeing* (BBC/Penguin, 1972); *About Looking* (Pantheon, 1980).
- **James Wood**, *How Fiction Works* (FSG, 2008); *The Broken Estate* (Random House, 1999).
- **Robert Hughes**, *The Shock of the New* (Knopf, 1981); *Nothing If Not Critical* (Knopf, 1990).
- **William Empson**, *Seven Types of Ambiguity* (Chatto, 1930).
- **Jorge Luis Borges**, *Other Inquisitions* (1952); "Kafka and His Precursors"; "The Argentine Writer and Tradition." Borges is the patron saint of the genre you are writing in.
- **Janet Malcolm**, *Forty-One False Starts* (FSG, 2013).

## Lines of inquiry — questions the thesis should answer

A reading list is only as useful as the questions it provokes. The following are the questions to keep open while reading, and to close (provisionally) in the thesis itself.

1. *What does canon mean if you accept that canons are made, not found?* This is the post-Guillory, post-Bourdieu question. If canonization is a sociological process, what is your relationship to it as a writer — collaborator, dissident, parasite, all three?

2. *What is the relationship between cult and canon? Sequence, opposition, or simultaneity?* The site's name implies a sequence (cult *to* canon). But Sontag and Eco suggest the categories can hold for the same object at once. Which is your view, and why?

3. *Is cult attention a virtue, a vice, or merely a phase?* Pater's "hard, gemlike flame" treats it as the highest mode of reading; Bloom treats it as adolescent. You will need to decide.

4. *Whose canon?* The single most important question after Said, Achebe, Morrison, Spivak, and Ngũgĩ. The thesis cannot avoid taking a position on whether *Cult to Canon* operates inside, against, or alongside the inherited Anglo-European canon — and if alongside, what its center of gravity is.

5. *What is the unit of canonization?* Books, films, sentences, scenes, gestures, channels, accounts, oeuvres, microgenres? The internet age has multiplied the available units, and any contemporary thesis has to specify.

6. *Is the writer of the site a critic, a curator, or both?* Curation is a 21st-c. word for what the Alexandrian librarians did; the question is whether you want to claim continuity with that lineage or invent a new role.

7. *What is the relationship between the personal canon and the public canon?* Calvino's *Why Read the Classics?* argues the question is the same question. Bloom argues they should be aligned. Sontag argues they are productively in tension. Where do you stand?

8. *What does the site's existence do?* Every writing apparatus has a theory of its own labor. A reading-list app has a theory; a digital garden has a theory; a magazine has a theory. The thesis should name what *Cult to Canon* is doing in the world — what kind of intervention it is.

9. *Why now?* The thesis should be able to answer what is specific to 2026 about doing this work — what conditions make it both possible and necessary. The answer is partly technical (the substrate), partly cultural (the apparatus is visibly broken or visibly mutating), and partly personal.

10. *What would falsify the project?* The most disciplined move in a thesis is to name the conditions under which you would close the site. If you cannot name them, the thesis is not yet honest.

## Reading order — a suggestion

If the list is intimidating, a possible path:

1. Start with **Calvino**, *Why Read the Classics?*, title essay only — short, foundational.
2. Then **Borges**, "Kafka and His Precursors" and "The Argentine Writer and Tradition" — both short.
3. Then **Eliot**, "Tradition and the Individual Talent" — short, the canonical position.
4. Then **Bourdieu**, "The Field of Cultural Production" essay (not the whole book yet) — the sociological pivot.
5. Then **Guillory**, *Cultural Capital*, introduction and chapter 1 — the critique.
6. Then **Sontag**, "Notes on 'Camp'" and "Against Interpretation" — the cult-side pivot.
7. Then **Achebe**, "An Image of Africa" — the decolonial pivot.
8. Then **Eco**, "Casablanca" — the cult-object theory.
9. Then **Fisher**, *Ghosts of My Life*, introduction — a working contemporary model.
10. Then **Calvino** again, the rest of *Why Read the Classics?* — now in light of everything above.

At that point, draft the thesis. Read deeper afterwards, not before. The thesis should be written from a position of partial knowledge, then revised against further reading — not delayed until reading is complete, because reading is never complete.
